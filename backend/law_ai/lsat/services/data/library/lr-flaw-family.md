## Descriptive, not prescriptive

A flaw question asks you to *describe* what went wrong. It does not ask you to fix it, rebut it, or supply what was missing. That distinction eliminates a whole class of attractive wrong answers: a choice that offers a good objection is answering a weaken question that was not asked. See [[concept:flaw-in-reasoning]].

Stems in this family: *vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that*, *the reasoning is flawed because it*, *questionable because the argument*, *fails to consider the possibility that*.

## Name it in your own words first

The method is one sentence long: **read the argument, say what is wrong with it in plain language, then find the choice that says that in exam language.**

This works because the exam's abstractions are hard to evaluate cold. Read five choices phrased as "takes for granted that", "presumes without warrant", "treats a condition as", and all five sound like they might describe something. Read them holding a specific defect you already named, and four fall away instantly.

The vocabulary that makes naming fast is in [[article:formal-fallacies-catalog]].

## Decoding the phrasings

The credited answer describes the defect abstractly. Learning the translations is most of the difficulty.

| Answer phrasing | What it means |
|---|---|
| *takes for granted that* | assumes without support that |
| *presumes, without providing justification, that* | same thing, longer |
| *treats a condition that is necessary as one that is sufficient* | conditional confusion |
| *infers a causal connection from a correlation* | the causal template |
| *draws a conclusion about a whole from evidence about a part* | composition |
| *relies on a term used in two different senses* | equivocation |
| *presupposes what it sets out to prove* | circular |
| *fails to consider that the reverse may hold* | reverse causation |
| *overlooks the possibility that* | ignores an alternative |

> [!example] Naming, then matching
> *Every employee who completed the ethics training reported feeling more confident about compliance. The training is therefore effective at improving compliance.*
>
> Name it: the evidence is about *self-reported confidence*, the conclusion is about *actual compliance*. Those are different things, and the argument slides from one to the other.
>
> Now the credited answer is easy to spot: *"treats evidence about employees' perceptions as if it established a fact about their behaviour."* Without the name, that choice competes with four others about sample size, self-selection, and the absence of a control group — all of which are also true of the argument.

## "True of the argument" is not the test

This is the family's defining trap, and it deserves its own habit.

Every flaw question contains at least one choice that accurately describes something the argument does — it *does* cite an expert, it *does* discuss only one city, it *does* use an example. Accuracy is not the criterion. The criterion is whether that feature is **why the argument fails**.

> [!trap] The two-part filter
> Run every surviving choice through both halves:
>
> 1. **Is it true of this argument?** If the argument never does this, eliminate. (Choices describing flaws the argument does not commit are the other half of the distractor set.)
> 2. **Is it the reason the conclusion does not follow?** If the argument would still fail with this fixed, or would still succeed with it present, eliminate.
>
> A choice must pass both. Most wrong answers pass exactly one, which is why they survive a careless read.

## When you cannot name it

Occasionally the defect will not come. Two fallbacks, in order:

- **Compare the conclusion's scope to the premises'.** Nine times out of ten the conclusion is broader, stronger, or about a different thing. That mismatch is usually the flaw.
- **Check for the two most common defects.** Necessary–sufficient confusion and correlation-to-cause account for a large share of the family. Test both explicitly before guessing.

## Key takeaways

- Flaw questions are descriptive: name the defect, do not repair the argument.
- Say what is wrong in plain language *before* reading the choices.
- Learn the abstract phrasings; matching plain language to abstraction is the real work.
- Two-part filter: true of the argument, and the reason it fails. Both, or eliminate.
- Stuck? Compare conclusion scope to premise scope, then check necessary–sufficient and causal.
