## The section

Two scored 35-minute sections, roughly 25 questions each. Each question is a short stimulus — usually 30 to 90 words — followed by one stem and five choices. Because both scored LR sections count, this section is close to half your score, and it is where a study plan should concentrate. See [[article:lsat-exam-architecture]].

Question difficulty is not uniform and is loosely ordered: the first eight or so are typically approachable, questions ten through twenty carry the weight, and the last few are often the hardest on the section. That ordering is what makes triage possible; see [[article:pacing-and-time-management]].

## The family map

Every LR question belongs to one of seven families, and the family determines the method. Learn to classify from the stem before reading anything else.

| Family | Typical stem language | What the credited answer does |
|---|---|---|
| Assumption | *depends on assuming*, *if which one is assumed* | Supplies an unstated link the argument needs |
| Strengthen / Weaken | *most strengthens*, *most seriously weakens* | Adds a new fact that helps or hurts the inference |
| Flaw | *vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that* | Names the defect in the reasoning |
| Inference | *must be true*, *most strongly supported* | Follows from the stimulus without adding anything |
| Structure | *main conclusion*, *role*, *method*, *point at issue* | Describes what a part of the argument does |
| Paradox / Evaluate | *resolve the discrepancy*, *most useful to know* | Reconciles two facts, or identifies a decisive test |
| Application | *principle*, *parallel*, *completes the argument* | Matches an abstract pattern to a concrete case |

Each family has its own article: [[article:lr-assumption-family]], [[article:lr-strengthen-and-weaken]], [[article:lr-flaw-family]], [[article:lr-inference-family]], [[article:lr-main-point-and-role]], [[article:lr-paradox-and-evaluate]], [[article:lr-parallel-family]], [[article:lr-principle-family]].

## The four-step routine

The same routine works across every family, and running it in order is what separates consistent scorers from streaky ones.

1. **Read the stem first.** Ten words tell you what job to do, and reading the stimulus without knowing the job means reading it twice.
2. **Read the stimulus for structure.** Find the conclusion, mark the support, notice the pivot. Not for content — for shape. See [[article:argument-anatomy]].
3. **Pre-phrase.** Say what the answer must accomplish, in your own words, before looking. On assumption, strengthen, weaken and flaw questions this is worth more than any elimination technique.
4. **Eliminate against the pre-phrase**, then choose. See [[article:answer-choice-elimination]].

> [!example] The routine on a hard item
> *Stem: The argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that it*
>
> *Stimulus: A recent audit found that departments using the new procurement software processed invoices 40% faster than departments still using the old system. The software vendor concludes that adopting the software will reduce processing times across the entire agency.*
>
> **Step 1:** flaw question — I need the defect, not a rebuttal.
> **Step 2:** conclusion is "adopting the software will reduce processing times agency-wide"; support is a 40% gap between departments that already chose it and those that did not.
> **Step 3:** pre-phrase — the departments that adopted early may differ from the rest in ways that explain the speed. Self-selection. The argument assumes the comparison groups are alike.
> **Step 4:** now scan for *"takes for granted that departments that adopted the software are representative of the agency as a whole"* and ignore the four choices about cost, training, and vendor bias.

## Common failure modes

- **Reading for content, not structure.** The subject matter is irrelevant. What matters is which sentence supports which.
- **Skipping the pre-phrase under time pressure.** This is exactly backwards: the pre-phrase is a time *saver*, because it makes four choices obviously wrong at a glance.
- **Not classifying the stem.** Answering a weaken question with a flaw answer is a specific, common, avoidable error.
- **Rereading the stimulus for each choice.** If you need the stimulus five times you did not extract its structure the first time.

> [!trap] Answering the argument instead of the question
> A stimulus makes a claim you find unconvincing, and a choice says something you agree with about why. That is not the question. Every stem defines a narrow job — supply the assumption, name the defect, add a fact that weakens. Agreement is not the criterion; doing the stated job is. This one habit accounts for a large fraction of missed LR questions at every score level.

## Key takeaways

- Two scored sections, roughly half the exam. Concentrate study time here.
- Seven families; classify from the stem before reading the stimulus.
- Stem, structure, pre-phrase, eliminate — in that order, every time.
- Difficulty rises through the section, which is what makes triage viable.
- The commonest error is doing a reasonable thing that the stem did not ask for.
