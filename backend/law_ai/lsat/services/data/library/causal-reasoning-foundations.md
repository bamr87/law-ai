## The shape of a causal argument

An enormous share of Logical Reasoning stimuli follow one template: the author observes that two things go together, and concludes that one causes the other.

```
Premise:     X and Y are correlated (or X preceded Y)
Conclusion:  X causes Y
```

Recognising this template is worth more than any individual technique, because the moment you see it you already know the four ways it can be attacked and what the credited answer will look like. See [[concept:causation-vs-correlation]].

## The four attacks

Every weaken answer to a causal argument is one of these, and every strengthen answer is one of them denied.

- **Alternative cause.** Some third factor Z causes Y instead. This is the most common credited weakener on the exam. See [[concept:alternative-cause]].
- **Reverse causation.** Y causes X, not the other way round. Especially available when the stimulus only establishes that the two occur together, never which came first.
- **Coincidence.** The correlation is chance, or the sample was too small or too odd to establish anything. See [[concept:sampling-flaw]].
- **Cause without effect, or effect without cause.** A case where X happened and Y did not, or Y happened with no X. A single well-chosen counterexample undercuts a general causal claim.

To **strengthen** the same argument, deny one of the four: rule out the alternative cause, establish the temporal order, add a controlled comparison, or show the effect tracks the cause across cases.

> [!example] One stimulus, four attacks
> *Employees at firms with open-plan offices report 30% more sick days than employees at firms with private offices. Open-plan layouts must therefore be damaging employee health.*
>
> **Alternative cause:** open-plan firms are concentrated in industries with younger workforces and more generous sick-leave policies, so days are *reported* more freely.
> **Reverse causation:** firms with high absenteeism restructure into open plans to improve supervision.
> **Coincidence or sampling:** the comparison covers eleven firms in one city over one winter.
> **Effect without cause:** private-office firms in the same industry show the same 30% gap year over year.
>
> Each of these is a plausible credited answer to "which most weakens". Generating them *before* reading the choices is the technique; see [[article:lr-strengthen-and-weaken]].

## Signals that a causal claim is being made

The conclusion does not have to contain the word "cause". Watch for:

- *leads to*, *produces*, *results in*, *is responsible for*, *explains*
- *promotes*, *reduces*, *prevents*, *contributes to*
- A recommendation that depends on causation: *to reduce Y, do less X* presumes X causes Y.

That last one is easy to miss. "The city should ban open-plan offices" is a causal argument wearing a policy hat, and the four attacks apply unchanged.

> [!trap] Attacking the premise
> A choice that denies the correlation itself — "employees at open-plan firms actually report fewer sick days" — feels devastating and is usually wrong. The exam expects you to accept the premises as given and attack the *inference*. A choice that contradicts a stated premise is more often a trap than a credited answer, because the argument's defect is the leap from correlation to cause, not the correlation.

## Where causation meets numbers

Causal stimuli lean heavily on statistics, and two moves recur often enough to name:

- **Percentage versus number.** A rise in the *rate* of something is not a rise in the *count*, and vice versa, unless the base population held steady. See [[article:lr-numbers-and-percentages]] and [[concept:percent-vs-number]].
- **Unrepresentative sample.** A study establishes something about the group studied. Whether it generalises depends on how that group was selected, which the stimulus frequently declines to tell you.

## Key takeaways

- Correlation-to-cause is the exam's most common argument template. Learn to see it instantly.
- Four attacks: alternative cause, reverse causation, coincidence or bad sample, and cause-without-effect.
- Strengthening is denying one of the four, most often ruling out an alternative cause.
- Causal claims hide inside recommendations and inside verbs like "leads to" and "explains".
- Attack the inference, not the premise; a choice that denies the data is usually a trap.
