## Global questions

*The main point of the passage is…* / *The passage is primarily concerned with…*

Answered from the **map**, not from any sentence. The credited answer covers the whole passage — including the pivot — at the right level of generality. See [[concept:main-point]].

Two failure modes: a choice that describes only one paragraph (too narrow) and a choice that describes the general topic without the author's angle (too broad). If the passage argued that a traditional view is incomplete, "the history of a traditional view" is too broad and "the sales figures for an 1847 treatise" is too narrow.

## Detail questions

*According to the passage…* / *The passage states that…*

Answered by **going back and reading the sentence**. These are the most reliably gettable questions in the section and the most commonly missed through overconfidence. The credited answer is a paraphrase of something explicitly stated; a choice that is true, sensible, and not stated is wrong. See [[concept:detail-question]].

## Inference questions

*The passage suggests that…* / *It can be inferred from the passage that…* / *The author would most likely agree that…*

The standard is what the passage **supports**, which is stricter than what it fails to contradict and looser than what it says outright. See [[concept:rc-inference]].

The reliable check is the same one from [[article:lr-inference-family]]: point at the text. If you cannot identify a sentence or two doing the supporting, the choice does not follow, however reasonable it sounds.

> [!example] Inference versus plausible extension
> *Text: "Although the charters were drafted in Latin, surviving marginalia suggest that town clerks routinely glossed key provisions in the vernacular for the benefit of councillors."*
>
> **Supported:** at least some councillors could not read Latin fluently. The gloss existed for their benefit; that is what the sentence is for.
>
> **Not supported, and attractive:** the councillors were illiterate. Nothing here says that — glossing Latin into the vernacular tells you about Latin, not about literacy.
>
> **Not supported:** the charters were unpopular. The sentence is silent on reception entirely.

## Function questions

*The author mentions the sales figures primarily in order to…* / *The second paragraph serves to…*

Answered from the **map**. The question is what a piece is doing structurally — supporting a claim, anticipating an objection, illustrating a distinction, conceding a point before rejecting it.

Read the sentence *before* and *after* the cited material. Function is always relative to its neighbours, and the answer is usually visible in the transition into or out of it.

## Attitude and tone

Answered by locating the author's evaluative language. Calibrate by degree, as [[article:rc-passage-types-and-mapping]] sets out, and remember that "neutral" or "expository" is a real and under-selected answer.

## Phrase in context

*In the second paragraph, the word "plastic" is used to mean…*

The passage's usage governs, not the dictionary's. Read the full sentence and the one before it, then substitute each candidate meaning and see which keeps the sentence coherent. The most common definition of the word is a deliberate lure. See [[concept:phrase-in-context]].

## Analogous situation

*Which one of the following is most analogous to the relationship described in the passage?*

Abstract the relationship, then match the abstraction — the same skill as [[article:lr-parallel-family]]. The choices are in unrelated domains on purpose, and matching on topic is always a mistake. See [[concept:analogous-situation]].

> [!trap] The half-right answer
> Reading Comprehension distractors are usually accurate about half the passage. A choice that correctly describes the traditional view but attributes it to the author; a choice that captures ¶3's evidence but calls it the main point; a choice that gets the author's position right and overstates its strength.
>
> The habit that catches these: read the **whole** choice, including the last few words. The exam hides the defect at the end more often than at the start, and readers who decide after the first clause miss it. See [[article:answer-choice-elimination]].

## Key takeaways

- Global and function questions are answered from the map; detail and inference from the text.
- Detail questions are gettable and are missed through overconfidence — go back and read.
- Inference standard: point at the supporting sentence, or eliminate.
- Phrase-in-context is governed by the passage's usage; the common meaning is the lure.
- Distractors are usually half right — read every choice to its end.
