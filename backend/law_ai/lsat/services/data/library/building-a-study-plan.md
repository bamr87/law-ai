## Start with a diagnostic, not with lessons

Take one full timed practice exam before you study anything. Not to get a good score — to get a *shape*. The diagnostic answers three questions no curriculum can answer for you: which section costs you the most raw points, whether your losses come from running out of time or from being wrong unhurried, and which question families you miss disproportionately.

Those three answers determine everything downstream. A test taker who misses fifteen Logical Reasoning questions because they never reach the last eight has a pacing problem and should not spend March learning conditional notation. A test taker who misses fifteen with time to spare has a method problem, and pacing drills will do nothing for them.

> [!example] Two identical scores, two opposite plans
> Both score 152. The first left nine questions blank across two LR sections and got 90% of what they attempted right. The second answered everything and got 70% right.
>
> The first needs triage and pacing: they already have the method and are being robbed by the clock. The second needs method: going faster would only produce wrong answers sooner. Handing both the same "eight-week LR course" wastes one of them entirely.

## The four phases

- **Phase 1 — Foundations (2 to 3 weeks).** Argument structure, conditional logic, causal reasoning. Untimed. The goal is that you can name what an argument is doing before you look at the choices. Start with [[article:argument-anatomy]] and [[article:conditional-logic-foundations]].
- **Phase 2 — Method by question type (4 to 8 weeks).** One family at a time: read the family guide, drill twenty to thirty items of only that family, review every one. This is where most of the score comes from, and where most plans skip ahead too early.
- **Phase 3 — Mixed drilling and pacing (3 to 6 weeks).** Full sections under time. The skill being built is not the reasoning — it is the triage and the clock discipline in [[article:pacing-and-time-management]].
- **Phase 4 — Simulation (3 to 6 weeks).** Full four-section exams, same time of day as your real administration, same breaks, no pausing. Sit at least four.

Phases overlap at the edges. They do not reorder: simulating before you have method just produces a stable record of the same mistakes.

## Volume, honestly

Roughly 250 to 300 hours is the common figure for a substantial improvement, spread over three to six months. Compressing that into six weeks does not work well, because the binding constraint is not hours — it is the number of *review cycles* your reasoning gets to run through. You need to be wrong, see why, and meet the same pattern again a week later.

A workable weekly shape for someone with a job:

| Day | Work |
|---|---|
| Two weekday evenings | 45–60 min drilling one question family, plus full review |
| Two weekday evenings | 30 min review of the error log, no new questions |
| One weekend morning | A full timed section or, in phase 4, a full exam |
| One weekend afternoon | Blind review of that section or exam |

Note the ratio. Review time is roughly equal to drilling time. Anyone whose plan is 90% new questions and 10% review is buying repetition of their own errors.

> [!trap] The three plan-killers
> **Score-chasing.** Taking practice exams weekly to watch the number. Exams are a measurement instrument and a poor teaching one; you have a finite supply and they teach least per hour.
>
> **Drilling without reviewing.** Two hundred questions skimmed teaches less than forty reviewed properly. See [[article:blind-review-method]].
>
> **Studying your strengths.** Logical Reasoning feels good when you are good at it. The error log exists to override that instinct; see [[article:error-log-and-diagnosis]].

## Adjusting without thrashing

Re-plan on trends, not on a single result. A practice score four points below your average is inside normal variance, as [[article:lsat-scoring-and-percentiles]] explains. Change the plan when three consecutive data points point the same way, or when the error log shows a family you have drilled twice and still miss at the same rate — that one means the *method* is wrong, not the volume.

## Key takeaways

- Diagnose first. The same score can mean a pacing problem or a method problem, and they need opposite plans.
- Four phases in order: foundations, method by type, mixed pacing, full simulation.
- Budget roughly 250–300 hours over three to six months; review time should roughly equal drilling time.
- Practice exams measure, they do not teach. Spend them sparingly and late.
- Re-plan on trends of three, never on a single test.
