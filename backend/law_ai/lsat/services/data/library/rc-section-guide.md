## The section

One scored 35-minute section: four passage sets, roughly 27 questions total. Three sets are a single passage of 450 to 550 words; one is a **comparative** set of two shorter passages on a common topic, which behaves differently enough to need its own treatment in [[article:rc-comparative-passages]].

Subject matter rotates across four rough areas — law, humanities, social science, natural science — and none of it requires background knowledge. A passage on cephalopod neurology and a passage on legal formalism are the same task wearing different vocabulary.

## The time budget

Roughly 8 minutes 45 seconds per set. The split that works for most people:

| Phase | Time | What you are doing |
|---|---|---|
| Read and map | 3:00 – 4:00 | Structure, not detail |
| Questions | 4:45 – 5:45 | Return to the text for each one |

Two failure modes sit either side of that budget. Reading for **retention** — trying to hold the content in memory — takes six minutes and still fails, because the questions ask about things nobody remembers. Reading for **speed** — skimming in ninety seconds — means every question becomes a search through unmapped text.

The target is reading for **structure**: after one pass you should know what each paragraph does, where the author's own view sits, and where to look for anything else. See [[article:rc-passage-types-and-mapping]].

## What the section actually tests

- **Structure.** What is this paragraph doing — introducing, complicating, conceding, refuting, illustrating?
- **Author's position.** Does the author endorse, reject, or withhold judgement, and how strongly? See [[concept:author-attitude]].
- **Attribution.** Whose view is this sentence? Passages routinely present two or three positions and the exam tests whether you tracked which is which.
- **Support.** What does the text license, and what does it merely fail to contradict? See [[concept:rc-inference]].

Note what is missing: recall. You are allowed to look. The section is open-book, and treating it that way is the difference between a manageable set and an unmanageable one.

> [!example] Reading for structure, in one pass
> A passage on the reception of a nineteenth-century legal treatise might map as:
>
> ```
> ¶1  Traditional view: the treatise was ignored in its time.
> ¶2  Evidence for the traditional view — sales figures, few reviews.
> ¶3  PIVOT. New archival work shows heavy citation in trial courts.
> ¶4  Author's position: influence was real but invisible to the
>     usual measures. Concedes the sales data is accurate.
> ```
>
> That map is four lines and about forty seconds of thought. It answers the main-point question outright, tells you the author's attitude, locates every detail question, and makes the "traditional view versus author's view" attribution questions trivial. Nothing about cephalopods or citation counts had to be memorised.

## Order and triage

Passages are not ordered by difficulty and you are not required to take them in order. Two habits pay:

- **Start with the set you can read fastest**, if the difference is obvious in ten seconds of glancing. A dense abstract passage taken last is a passage you take with a clearer sense of your remaining time.
- **Never abandon a set you have already read.** The reading is the expensive part; the questions are comparatively cheap. Leaving a mapped passage to start an unmapped one is the worst possible trade. See [[article:guessing-and-triage]].

> [!trap] Answering from memory
> The single most expensive habit in this section is answering a detail or inference question from what you remember reading. Memory reconstructs plausibly and the exam's wrong answers are built out of plausible reconstructions.
>
> If a question is about what the passage says, go back and read the sentence. Every time. The map exists so that going back costs eight seconds instead of ninety.

## Key takeaways

- One section, four sets, roughly 8:45 each — about 3:30 reading, 5:00 answering.
- Read for structure and attribution, not for retention. The section is open-book.
- A four-line paragraph map answers the global questions and locates the rest.
- Take the easiest set first if it is obvious; never abandon a set you have already read.
- Go back to the text for every question that is about the text.
