## What the exam is for

The LSAT does not test law. It tests four narrow abilities that law school assumes you already have: reading dense prose for structure rather than for facts, evaluating whether a conclusion actually follows from what preceded it, holding a set of formal constraints in your head while you draw consequences from them, and writing a position you can defend under time pressure.

That framing matters more than it sounds. Almost every wrong answer a beginner picks is *true*, or *plausible*, or *something the passage would probably agree with*. Almost none of them do the specific job the question asked for. The exam is a test of task discipline as much as of reasoning, and every technique in this library is downstream of that one fact.

## The sections

A scored administration is four 35-minute sections plus a separately-delivered writing sample. Three of the four count toward your score; one does not.

| Section | Scored | Length | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Logical Reasoning | Two sections, both scored | 35 min each | ~25 short arguments, one question each |
| Reading Comprehension | One section, scored | 35 min | 4 passage sets, ~27 questions total |
| Unscored variable section | No | 35 min | An extra LR or RC section being pretested |
| Argumentative Writing | Not scored numerically | 35 min | One prompt, submitted separately |

Two consequences follow immediately. First, **Logical Reasoning is roughly half your score** — two of the three scored sections. A point of LR improvement is worth about twice a point of RC improvement, which is why a study plan that splits time evenly between them is already misallocated. Second, **you cannot identify the unscored section while you are taking it**, and people who try are reliably wrong. Treat all four as scored; the alternative is throwing away a scored section on a hunch.

> [!example] What "the variable section" costs people
> A test taker sits two Logical Reasoning sections and decides the second one "felt like a pretest" because the questions seemed strange. They coast through it at half effort. The strange-feeling section was scored; the ordinary-feeling first one was the pretest. They have just discarded a quarter of their exam on a feeling.
>
> There is no tell. The only correct policy is to treat every section as the one that counts.

Logic Games — formally Analytical Reasoning — was removed from the scored exam in August 2024. It survives in this library and in the drill bank as a reasoning trainer, not as a scored section: the constraint-tracking it teaches transfers directly to conditional questions in Logical Reasoning, and the boards are the cleanest available practice at holding several rules in working memory at once. See [[article:lg-section-guide]] for how to use it now that it no longer counts.

## What each 35 minutes actually demands

The clock is the exam's real difficulty. Every section is written so that a careful, unhurried reader would score near the top and almost nobody has time to be one.

- **Logical Reasoning** gives you about 1 minute 25 seconds per question, and the questions are not equally hard. The skill is not "go faster" but "spend 45 seconds on the easy ones so you can spend two and a half minutes on the hard ones". See [[article:pacing-and-time-management]].
- **Reading Comprehension** gives you roughly 8 minutes 45 seconds per passage set, of which 3 to 4 minutes is reading and the rest is answering. Readers who try to memorise the passage run out of time on the questions; readers who skim have to re-read for every question. See [[article:rc-passage-types-and-mapping]].
- **Argumentative Writing** is untimed in the sense that nobody scores it out of a hundred, and completely load-bearing in the sense that schools read it and an unsubmitted sample makes your file incomplete. See [[article:writing-section-guide]].

## What it does not test

Knowing what is *out of scope* prevents a large class of wrong answers.

- **Outside knowledge.** If an answer requires a fact from your own life, it is wrong. The passage or stimulus supplies everything.
- **Real-world truth.** A stimulus can assert something false. Your job is what follows *from it*, not whether it is true.
- **Speed reading.** Comprehension per minute beats words per minute on every measured section.
- **Legal doctrine.** Nothing on the exam requires knowing a single case, rule, or statute.

> [!trap] The plausibility trap
> The most common wrong answer on the entire exam is the choice that is reasonable, sensible, and something a thoughtful person would agree with — and that does not do what the question asked. "Which one most weakens the argument" is not "which one sounds like a good objection". Discipline about the question stem is the single highest-return habit available; [[article:answer-choice-elimination]] is built around it.

## Key takeaways

- Four 35-minute sections; three scored, one unscored and unidentifiable. Treat all four as real.
- Logical Reasoning is two of the three scored sections — roughly half the score, and where study time should concentrate.
- The exam tests task discipline under a clock, not knowledge; every section is doable untimed.
- Logic Games left the scored exam in August 2024 but remains the best available trainer for constraint tracking.
- Wrong answers are usually plausible. Right answers do exactly what the stem asked.
