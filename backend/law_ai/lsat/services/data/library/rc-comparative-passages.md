## What is different

One of the four sets each section is comparative: two shorter passages, A and B, on a shared topic, followed by questions that are mostly about the **relationship** between them. See [[concept:rc-comparative]].

The reading is not harder. The mistake is treating it as two ordinary passages and mapping each in isolation, which produces two good maps and no answer to the questions that actually appear.

## Map the relationship

After reading both, you want four things:

- **What each passage claims.** One sentence each.
- **The relationship.** Does B respond to A, extend it, illustrate it, narrow it, or address the same topic independently?
- **Points of agreement.** What would both authors accept?
- **Points of disagreement.** What does one assert that the other denies?

```
A:   Municipal charters spread through trade networks.
B:   Charters spread through ecclesiastical administration.
Rel: Independent competing explanations of the same pattern;
     B does not mention A.
Agree:   the pattern is uneven; regional variation is real.
Disagree: which mechanism is primary.
Scope:   A covers 1100–1300; B covers 1150–1250 only.
```

That last line matters more than it looks. Comparative passages frequently differ in **scope** — one broader, one narrower, one general and one a case study — and scope differences are a common credited answer to relationship questions.

## The relationship types

| Relationship | Signature |
|---|---|
| B rebuts A | B names or clearly targets A's position |
| B extends A | Same framework, new application or later period |
| B narrows A | Agrees generally, restricts the domain |
| Independent parallel | Same topic, no engagement, possibly compatible |
| Different level | A is theoretical, B is a concrete case |

Passages that never mention each other are the norm, not the exception. "B rebuts A" is over-selected by test takers and is correct less often than it feels.

## The question types unique to this set

- **Both authors would agree that…** Requires each passage to support the claim. A statement only A makes fails, even if B would probably accept it — *probably accept* is not *supports*. This is the same two-sided discipline as point-at-issue in [[article:lr-main-point-and-role]].
- **The authors disagree about whether…** Both must take a position, and the positions must conflict.
- **Passage B relates to Passage A in that…** The relationship map answers it directly.
- **A term used in both passages…** Frequently the two authors use the same word differently, and the question is testing exactly that.

> [!example] Agreement that is not agreement
> *A argues that expanding public transit reduces urban emissions and should be funded through congestion charges. B argues that transit expansion reduces emissions only when paired with parking restrictions, and is silent on funding.*
>
> **"Both authors would agree that transit expansion can reduce emissions"** — supported. A asserts it; B asserts it conditionally, which still commits B to the "can".
>
> **"Both authors would agree that congestion charges are the appropriate funding mechanism"** — not supported. B never addresses funding. Silence is not agreement, and this is the family's characteristic trap.
>
> **"The authors disagree about whether transit expansion reduces emissions"** — false. They disagree about the conditions, not the effect.

> [!trap] Treating silence as assent
> The most frequent error on comparative sets is inferring one author's position on a topic the other raised. If Passage B never discusses funding, B has no position on funding, and no choice about B's view of funding can be supported.
>
> Before selecting an agreement or disagreement answer, locate the supporting text in **both** passages. If you can only point to one, eliminate.

## Reading order

Read A, map it in one line, read B, map it in one line, then spend fifteen seconds on the relationship before touching the questions. Do not read both and then try to reconstruct — the relationship is what the set is about, and building it while B is fresh is much faster than rebuilding it later.

## Key takeaways

- Map the relationship, not two separate passages.
- Record: each claim, the relationship type, agreements, disagreements, and scope differences.
- Passages that never engage each other are the norm; "B rebuts A" is over-selected.
- Agreement and disagreement questions need supporting text in *both* passages.
- Silence is not assent — the single most common trap in the set.
