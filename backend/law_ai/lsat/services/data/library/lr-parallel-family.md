## What has to match

*Which one of the following arguments is most similar in its pattern of reasoning to the argument above?* You are matching **structure**, and structure means four things:

- **The logical form** — conditional chain, causal claim, generalisation from a sample, analogy.
- **The conclusion type** — a prediction matches a prediction, a recommendation matches a recommendation, a claim of fact matches a claim of fact.
- **The strength of the language** — *must* matches *must*, *probably* matches *probably*. A stimulus concluding "may well be" does not match a choice concluding "certainly is".
- **The validity** — a valid argument matches a valid one; an invalid argument matches one that fails *the same way*.

What does **not** have to match: subject matter. In fact the exam deliberately changes the topic, and choices that keep the stimulus's topic are usually traps. See [[concept:parallel-reasoning]].

## Abstract before you read the choices

Reduce the stimulus to a skeleton using letters. This is not optional decoration — it is the only way to compare six arguments without rereading each one three times.

```
Stimulus: Every accredited programme requires a clinical year.
          Fairhaven does not require a clinical year.
          So Fairhaven is not accredited.

Skeleton: All A are B.  X is not B.  Therefore X is not A.
          (valid — contrapositive)
```

Now scan the choices for that shape. Any choice concluding "X is not A" from "X is not B" where the rule ran `A → B` matches. A choice concluding "X is A" because "X is B" is affirming the consequent — a different, invalid form.

> [!example] Matching a flawed pattern
> *Stimulus: Most of the firm's successful appeals were argued by senior partners. Novak is a senior partner. So Novak has probably argued a successful appeal.*
>
> Skeleton: *Most A are B. X is B. Therefore X is probably A.* The flaw is a reversal of a "most" statement — most successes came from senior partners, which says nothing about what share of senior partners produced a success. See [[article:quantifiers-and-overlap]].
>
> The credited choice will run the same reversal on a different topic: *"Most of the museum's stolen works were taken at night. This vase was taken at night. So it was probably one of the stolen works."* Same defect, same "probably", different subject.
>
> A choice with a genuine flaw of a different kind — say a causal reversal — does not match, however wrong it is.

## Parallel flaw

Same procedure, one addition: **name the flaw first**, using [[article:formal-fallacies-catalog]]. Then find the choice that commits *that* defect. A choice that is flawed differently is wrong, and the exam always supplies two or three of them.

The most common near-miss: a choice with the right topic-shape but a different error, or a choice that is valid when the stimulus was flawed. Checking validity explicitly catches both.

> [!trap] Matching on content
> A choice about the same subject as the stimulus — both about hospitals, both about universities — is a deliberate lure. Topic similarity is evidence of nothing. Cover the nouns with letters and the resemblance evaporates.

## When to skip

Parallel questions are the most time-expensive on the section: six arguments to analyse instead of one. If you are behind pace, they are the correct thing to leave.

The practical rule from [[article:pacing-and-time-management]]: if a parallel question appears while you are behind, mark a letter, flag it, and return only if time remains. Two ordinary questions answered carefully are worth more than one parallel question answered in four minutes.

> [!tip] Eliminate on conclusion type first
> Before doing any structural work, read only the *last sentence* of each choice. Conclusion type and strength eliminate two or three choices in under fifteen seconds, and you only have to abstract the survivors.

## Key takeaways

- Match logical form, conclusion type, language strength, and validity — never subject matter.
- Abstract the stimulus to letters before touching the choices.
- On parallel flaw, name the defect first, then find the choice committing that same defect.
- Same-topic choices are lures; cover the nouns.
- Highest time cost on the section — the correct question to skip when behind.
