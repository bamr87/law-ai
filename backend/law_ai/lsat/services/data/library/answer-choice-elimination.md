## Eliminate, do not select

The exam is built so that four choices are wrong for identifiable reasons and one is right for a reason that is often subtle. Hunting for the right answer means evaluating five candidates against a vague standard. Eliminating means applying four specific tests, each of which is easier.

Concretely: read all five choices before committing. Test takers who select the first choice that sounds good miss a specific, common item — the one where choice B is attractive and choice D is correct.

## The elimination pass

1. **Cross out anything that fails on shape.** Wrong question type answered, wrong direction, wrong kind of sentence. This is fast and removes one or two.
2. **Cross out anything requiring outside information.** If you have to supply a fact, it is not supported.
3. **Cross out anything about the wrong object.** Weakens a subsidiary claim, describes the wrong paragraph, addresses a different conclusion.
4. **Compare the survivors on a single dimension.** Usually scope or strength. Name the dimension explicitly rather than re-reading both hoping one feels better.

Step 4 is where accuracy is won. "I'll read them both again" is not a method. "B says *all*, D says *some*, and the stimulus only supports *some*" is.

## Language patterns in wrong answers

- **Extreme quantifiers** — *all*, *none*, *always*, *never*, *only*, *must*. Usually too strong on inference and RC questions.
- **New terms.** A concept appearing in the choice and nowhere in the stimulus. Almost always a distractor.
- **Reversed relationships.** The right terms in the wrong direction, especially with conditionals. See [[article:conditional-logic-foundations]].
- **True but irrelevant.** Accurate about the passage, unresponsive to the stem. The single most common wrong-answer type in Reading Comprehension.
- **Half-right, half-wrong.** Correct through the first clause and defective after. The defect is usually at the end.

That last one is why the rule is: **read every choice to its final word.** The exam knows people commit early.

> [!example] Where the defect hides
> *Stem: which one of the following most accurately states the main point?*
>
> *Choice: "The traditional account of the treatise's reception is inaccurate because it relies on sales figures, which recent archival evidence has shown to be fabricated."*
>
> The first two thirds are right — the traditional account is challenged, and it did rest on sales figures. The final clause is the defect: the passage said the sales figures were *accurate but incomplete as a measure of influence*, not fabricated.
>
> A reader who commits after "relies on sales figures" picks this. A reader who finishes the sentence eliminates it in a second.

## Comparing the last two

When two choices survive, the difference is almost always one of:

- **Scope** — one covers more than the text supports, or less than the question asked.
- **Strength** — one asserts more confidently than the evidence licenses.
- **Object** — they are about different things: the argument versus a premise, the author versus a cited theorist.
- **Direction** — one has the causal or conditional relationship reversed.

Name which of the four is in play. If you cannot name it, you are probably comparing on feel, and feel is what the distractor was built for.

> [!trap] Falling in love with a choice
> You read choice B, it matches your pre-phrase, and you stop reading. The exam builds this trap constantly: B is close to the pre-phrase and D is the pre-phrase.
>
> The pre-phrase is a filter, not an answer. It tells you what to look for. Check all five against it, then choose the best match — not the first acceptable one.

## After the exam, review your eliminations

In review, do not just check the right answer. For every wrong choice, state the reason it fails, in the vocabulary above. The reasons repeat, and after fifty questions you will recognise the distractor patterns on sight. That is the core of [[article:blind-review-method]].

## Key takeaways

- Eliminate against four specific tests rather than hunting for the right answer.
- Read all five choices, every time, to the final word — defects hide at the end.
- Watch for extremes, new terms, reversals, true-but-irrelevant, and half-right choices.
- When two survive, name the dimension — scope, strength, object, or direction.
- In review, articulate why each wrong choice is wrong. The patterns repeat.
