## Uniform pacing is the wrong target

"About 1 minute 25 per Logical Reasoning question" is an average, and treating it as a budget is the mistake that defines mid-range scores. The questions are not equally hard and are loosely ordered by difficulty, so spending equal time on each guarantees you overspend on the easy ones and underspend on the hard ones.

The correct target is **unequal**: 45 to 60 seconds on early questions, 90 seconds on the middle, and two to three minutes available for the handful of genuinely hard items late in the section. The time for the hard ones is bought from the easy ones, and it can only be bought there.

| Question range | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1–10 | 45–60 sec each | Easier by design; this is where time is banked |
| 11–18 | 75–95 sec each | The modal difficulty |
| 19–25 | Whatever remains | Hardest items; needs the banked time |

## Checkpoints, not a stopwatch

Checking the clock after every question is a tax on attention and produces anxiety without information. Use two or three **checkpoints** instead.

For a 25-question Logical Reasoning section starting at 35:00:

- **After question 10** — roughly 24 minutes should remain.
- **After question 18** — roughly 12 minutes should remain.

For Reading Comprehension:

- **After the second passage set** — roughly 17 minutes should remain.

At a checkpoint you do exactly one thing: compare, and if you are behind, change behaviour immediately rather than resolving to go faster. "Going faster" is not an action. Skipping the next long question is.

> [!example] Recovering at a checkpoint
> You reach question 10 with 20 minutes left instead of 24. You are four minutes behind, with fifteen questions to go.
>
> Wrong response: read faster and try to make it up gradually. This produces rushed reading on the hardest part of the section and typically loses more than the four minutes.
>
> Right response: buy the time back in one transaction. Scan questions 11 to 15 and find the one longest stimulus or the one parallel-reasoning item. Guess it, flag it, move on. You have recovered two to three minutes in twenty seconds, and you spend the rest of the section at proper speed instead of rushing fifteen questions.

## The two-pass approach

Some test takers do better with a deliberate two-pass structure, especially in Logical Reasoning.

**First pass:** answer everything you can do at normal speed. When a question resists — you have read the stimulus twice and have no pre-phrase — mark a letter, flag it, and move on. Do not linger.

**Second pass:** return to the flagged questions with whatever time remains, hardest last.

The advantage is that no easy question at the end of the section goes unseen because a hard question in the middle ate the clock. The cost is the overhead of returning. It suits people whose error log shows late-section questions missed for lack of time rather than lack of method — see [[article:error-log-and-diagnosis]].

> [!trap] The sunk-cost minute
> You have spent ninety seconds on a question and have it down to two choices. Every additional thirty seconds feels justified because you are so close.
>
> You are not close. Down-to-two after ninety seconds means the distinction is subtle, and subtle distinctions rarely resolve through staring. Guessing between two choices is a 50% shot that costs zero further seconds; continuing is a maybe-70% shot that costs sixty and takes them from a question you would have answered correctly.
>
> The rule that beats intuition: **when you notice you have spent too long, you already have.** Choose between the survivors, flag it, move.

## Building pace in practice

Pacing is trained, not decided. Three stages:

1. **Untimed accuracy first.** There is no point pacing a method you do not have.
2. **Then generous timing** — 40 minutes for a 35-minute section — until accuracy holds.
3. **Then real timing**, with checkpoints, until the checkpoint numbers stop surprising you.

Skipping to stage three is the standard mistake and produces a stable habit of rushing.

## Key takeaways

- Target unequal times: bank seconds on early questions to spend on late ones.
- Use two or three checkpoints, not a per-question stopwatch.
- Behind at a checkpoint? Buy the time back in one transaction by skipping a long question.
- The sunk-cost minute is the biggest single leak; when you notice, you are already over.
- Train pace in three stages — untimed, generous, real. Skipping stages trains rushing.
