## What it is and why it matters

LSAT Argumentative Writing is completed separately from the multiple-choice exam, on your own computer, under proctoring. It is **not scored numerically** and does not affect your 120–180 result. It is sent to every school you apply to, unedited, and your file is incomplete until you submit it.

That combination — unscored but read, and blocking — is why it gets neglected and why neglecting it is a mistake. Admissions readers use it as a writing sample that they know you produced alone, under time, without editing help. It is the one piece of your application whose authorship is certain.

## What the prompt gives you

A prompt presents a decision with two defensible positions, a short scenario, and several stated **perspectives** or criteria bearing on the choice. There is no correct answer. Both positions are supportable, and the prompt is constructed so that each has real costs.

```
Scenario:   A city must choose between two uses for a vacant lot.
Position A: A public park.
Position B: Mixed-income housing.
Perspectives: housing shortage severity; existing green space
              per resident; construction timeline; maintenance cost.
```

You have a short pre-writing period and then 35 minutes to draft. Nothing is scored for length, and nobody is counting words.

## What readers are actually looking for

- **A position, stated early and held.** The prompt asks you to choose. An essay that surveys both sides and declines to decide has not done the task. See [[concept:thesis-position]].
- **Reasons connected to the criteria.** Not assertion — argument. Each reason should tie a fact from the scenario to your position through a stated warrant. See [[concept:warrant]].
- **Honest engagement with the other side.** The strongest essays name the best argument *against* their position and answer it. See [[concept:counterargument-response]].
- **Scope discipline.** Claims you can defend in 35 minutes, qualified where they need qualifying. See [[concept:concession-and-scope]].
- **Organisation a reader can follow.** Paragraphs that each do one job. See [[concept:argument-organization]].

Not on the list: vocabulary, sentence variety, rhetorical flourish, or outside knowledge. Nothing you know about urban planning helps; everything the prompt gave you does.

> [!example] Committing versus surveying
> **Surveying (weak opening):** *"There are strong arguments on both sides of this difficult question. A park would provide green space, while housing would address a real need. Both considerations deserve weight."*
>
> Two sentences in and the reader knows nothing about what you think.
>
> **Committing (strong opening):** *"The city should build the housing. The park's benefits are real but incremental — the district already exceeds the citywide average for green space per resident — while the housing addresses a shortage the scenario describes as acute. When one option addresses a severe deficiency and the other improves an existing adequacy, the severe deficiency governs."*
>
> The position is stated, the criteria are engaged, and a decision rule is offered that the rest of the essay can apply. That last move — supplying the principle by which you weighed the options — is what separates a strong sample from a competent one.

## The 35 minutes

| Minutes | Work |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Read the perspectives, pick a side, list your two strongest reasons and the strongest objection |
| 5–28 | Draft: thesis, reason, reason, counterargument and response, close |
| 28–35 | Read once for clarity and for sentences that say less than you meant |

Planning is not optional overhead. Five minutes of structure produces a better essay than five extra minutes of drafting, because the failure mode here is not running short — it is an essay that wanders.

> [!trap] Choosing the side you believe
> Pick the position you can **argue better**, not the one you agree with. They are frequently different, and the prompt is designed so that both are defensible.
>
> If one side has more support in the stated perspectives, take that side even if your instinct runs the other way. Nobody is reading this to learn your views on municipal land use.

## Key takeaways

- Unscored, but sent to every school and required for a complete file.
- Take a position in the first two sentences and hold it.
- Argue from the stated perspectives; outside knowledge earns nothing.
- Name the strongest objection and answer it — surveying both sides without deciding fails the task.
- Plan for five minutes. The failure mode is wandering, not running short.
