## Raw score to scaled score

Your **raw score** is the number of questions you answered correctly across the three scored sections — roughly 75 to 77 of them. Nothing is subtracted for a wrong answer. That single fact has a strategic consequence bigger than most people give it: **there is never a reason to leave a bubble empty**. See [[article:guessing-and-triage]].

The raw score is then converted to a **scaled score** from 120 to 180 using a conversion table built for that specific exam form. The table is set before anyone sits the exam, by pretesting questions in the unscored variable section. This is why "the curve" is a misnomer — you are not competing against the people in the room with you. An easier form needs more raw points for the same scaled score; a harder form needs fewer. The conversion is a difficulty adjustment, not a ranking.

## The scale is not evenly spaced

Percentiles are what schools actually read, and they are wildly non-linear. The distribution bunches in the middle, so the same one-question improvement buys very different amounts depending on where you start.

| Scaled score | Approximate percentile | Roughly what one extra point moves |
|---|---|---|
| 150 | ~44th | About 3 percentile points |
| 160 | ~80th | About 2 percentile points |
| 165 | ~89th | About 1.5 percentile points |
| 170 | ~97th | Under 1 percentile point |
| 175 | ~99th | A fraction of a point |

Percentiles shift year to year and LSAC publishes the current table; the shape, not the exact figures, is what to plan around.

> [!example] Where the effort pays
> Two test takers each find three more correct answers through a month of work. The first moves from 149 to 152 and climbs roughly nine percentile points — a different admissions conversation. The second moves from 172 to 175 and climbs about one. Same work, very different return.
>
> This is not an argument against high scorers improving. It is an argument for knowing which curve you are on, and for a mid-scorer to expect visible returns rather than assuming the exam is immovable.

## What this means for how you study

- **Below the mid-150s**, the fastest points are usually not in exotic question types. They are in finishing sections you currently do not finish, and in the easy questions you rush past to reach hard ones you were never going to get. Read [[article:pacing-and-time-management]] before drilling anything.
- **In the 160s**, the profile is usually uneven: one section, or two or three question families, carries almost all the loss. An error log makes that visible in a week; see [[article:error-log-and-diagnosis]].
- **Above 170**, remaining misses are typically single hard items per section, and the work shifts from method to precision — the discipline in [[article:blind-review-method]] and [[article:answer-choice-elimination]].

> [!tip] Track raw, not scaled
> Scaled scores across different practice forms are not directly comparable, and the noise between two forms can exceed a month of real improvement. Track **raw correct per section** over time. It is the number that actually moves, and it tells you *where* it moved.

## Score bands, not scores

A single administration has measurement error of roughly plus or minus three points. Two people whose scores differ by two points are, statistically, indistinguishable. Treat your result as a band rather than a value: a 164 means "roughly 161 to 167", and a practice test that comes back four points below your average is inside normal variance rather than evidence that something broke.

The practical version of this: never rewrite a study plan on one practice test. Change it on a trend of three.

## Key takeaways

- No penalty for wrong answers, so every bubble gets filled — always.
- Scaled scores come from a per-form conversion table set in advance; you are not graded against the room.
- Percentiles are non-linear: a point is worth several times more in the low 150s than above 170.
- Track raw correct per section, not scaled scores, to see real movement.
- One administration carries roughly plus or minus three points of measurement error. Read scores as bands.
