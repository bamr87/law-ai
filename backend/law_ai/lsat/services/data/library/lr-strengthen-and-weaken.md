## What the family actually asks

*Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the argument?* You are handed a new fact and asked whether it makes the conclusion harder or easier to accept. Two constraints follow, and almost every miss in this family violates one of them.

**You accept the premises.** A choice that denies a stated fact is attacking the wrong thing. The argument's vulnerability lives in the *move* from premises to conclusion. See [[concept:weaken]].

**You attack the specific conclusion.** Not the topic, not the author's politics, not a related claim. If the conclusion is "the programme should be expanded", a fact showing the programme is expensive weakens only if cost bears on the stated reasoning.

## The causal template

The large majority of strengthen and weaken stimuli are causal, and [[article:causal-reasoning-foundations]] gives the four attacks. Applied here:

| To weaken a causal claim | To strengthen it |
|---|---|
| Introduce an alternative cause | Rule out the alternative cause |
| Reverse the causal order | Establish that the cause preceded the effect |
| Show the sample was unrepresentative | Show the comparison group was controlled |
| Show the cause without the effect | Show the effect tracks the cause across cases |

Pre-phrasing from this table before reading the choices is the single most effective habit in the family. You will frequently find you predicted the credited answer almost word for word.

> [!example] Pre-phrasing a weakener
> *Since the county introduced its mandatory recycling programme, household waste sent to landfill has fallen by 22%. The programme is clearly working.*
>
> Before reading choices, generate: (a) something else changed at the same time — a new landfill fee, a large employer leaving town; (b) waste is being diverted somewhere other than recycling, such as illegal dumping; (c) the 22% is a rate and the population shrank.
>
> A choice reading *"During the same period, the county began charging households by the bag for landfill collection"* is attack (a) — an alternative cause. That is the credited answer, and you had it before you read a word of the choices.

## Non-causal arguments

Not every stimulus is causal. The other common shapes:

- **Argument by analogy.** Weaken by showing the two cases differ in a relevant respect; strengthen by showing they are alike where it matters.
- **Argument from a sample or survey.** Weaken by attacking representativeness, response rates, or self-reporting. See [[concept:sampling-flaw]].
- **Plan or recommendation.** Weaken by showing the plan will not achieve its goal, will cost more than it saves, or has a side effect that defeats the purpose. Strengthen by ruling those out.
- **Prediction.** Weaken by identifying a condition that has changed since the trend was observed.

## Degree decides close calls

When two choices both point the right direction, strength settles it. On weaken questions, "some evidence suggests" loses to "in every documented case"; on strengthen questions, a choice that eliminates a possibility outright beats one that makes it less likely.

But strength only matters *after* relevance. A very strong statement about something the conclusion does not depend on does nothing, and the exam builds distractors exactly there: forceful language attached to an irrelevant claim.

> [!trap] The "weakens something" trap
> A choice damages a subsidiary claim, an example the author used, or the author's credibility — and leaves the main conclusion untouched. It genuinely weakens *something*, which is why it survives elimination.
>
> The check is mechanical: state the main conclusion aloud, then ask whether this choice makes *that sentence* less likely. If you have to route through two inferences to explain how, it is probably the trap.

> [!drill] Both directions on one stimulus
> Take any causal weaken item, such as [[question:lr-002]], and after answering, write the strengthener for the same argument. Forcing yourself to produce both makes the four-attack structure automatic, and strengthen questions stop feeling like a separate skill.

## Key takeaways

- Accept the premises; attack or defend the inference to the stated conclusion.
- Most stimuli are causal — pre-phrase from the four attacks before reading choices.
- Non-causal shapes: analogy, sample, plan, prediction, each with its own weak point.
- Relevance first, then degree. Strong language on an irrelevant claim does nothing.
- Beware the choice that weakens a subsidiary point and leaves the conclusion standing.
