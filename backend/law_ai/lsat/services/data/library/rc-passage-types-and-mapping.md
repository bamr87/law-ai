## What a map is

A map is not notes. Notes record content; a map records **function**. For each paragraph you want one short phrase answering "what is this paragraph *doing*", plus a mark showing whose view it carries.

```
¶1  problem + traditional answer
¶2  evidence for traditional answer
¶3  PIVOT — new evidence undercuts it
¶4  AUTHOR: revised account, concedes ¶2 data
```

Four lines, written or held in your head, produced during a single read. That map is what makes the section open-book in practice rather than in theory. See [[concept:rc-structure]].

## What to mark as you read

Keep annotation minimal — heavy underlining is re-reading in disguise. Four marks cover almost everything:

- **Pivots.** *But*, *however*, *yet*, *although*, *nevertheless*. These are where structure turns, and questions cluster around them.
- **Attribution shifts.** *Proponents argue*, *critics contend*, *the traditional view holds*, *recent scholarship suggests*. Bracket whose view starts here.
- **The author's own voice.** Evaluative language — *unpersuasive*, *compelling*, *overstated*, *fails to account for* — marks where the author stops reporting and starts judging. This is the most valuable thing in the passage.
- **Lists and enumerations.** *First… second…*, *three factors*. Detail questions live here.

Do not mark: names, dates, technical terms, statistics. You can find those in seconds when a question asks, and marking them costs attention you need for structure.

## The recurring shapes

Most passages follow one of a handful of structures. Recognising which one you are in after the first paragraph accelerates everything after.

| Shape | Signature | Where the author usually is |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional view, then challenge | ¶1 states an accepted account, a pivot arrives | With the challenge, often partially |
| Phenomenon, then competing explanations | Two or three theories presented in turn | Favouring one, or withholding |
| Problem, then proposed solution | A difficulty is set out, an approach offered | Evaluating the approach |
| Figure or work, then reassessment | A person or text and its reputation | Revising the reputation |
| Two-part development | A concept, then its application or consequence | Explaining, low-heat |

> [!example] Mapping a competing-explanations passage
> *A passage on why certain medieval towns adopted written charters:*
>
> ```
> ¶1  Phenomenon: charters spread unevenly across regions.
> ¶2  Explanation A — trade volume. Presented, some support.
> ¶3  Explanation B — proximity to episcopal courts. Stronger
>     evidence cited.
> ¶4  AUTHOR: B is better supported but A is not eliminated;
>     the two may interact. Tone: measured, favours B.
> ```
>
> Predict the questions before reading them. There will be a main-point question (answer: B better explains the pattern, though A retains some force), an attitude question (measured endorsement of B, not dismissal of A), a detail question about the evidence in ¶3, and at least one trap answer that overstates the author's rejection of A.
>
> That prediction is worth roughly a minute across the set.

## Author attitude, precisely

Attitude questions are decided by *degree*, and the exam scores the difference between "critical" and "dismissive". Calibrate:

- **Strong positive:** *compelling*, *decisive*, *establishes*.
- **Qualified positive:** *plausible*, *promising*, *a useful corrective*.
- **Neutral or expository:** the author reports without evaluating. This is common and under-selected.
- **Qualified negative:** *overstated*, *incomplete*, *fails to account for*.
- **Strong negative:** *untenable*, *baseless*, *thoroughly discredited*.

Most LSAT authors are qualified, not strong. An answer choice using an extreme word is usually wrong for that reason alone. See [[concept:rc-tone]].

> [!trap] Mapping content instead of function
> A map that reads "¶2: sales figures, 1847, low reviews" is notes, and it will not help you. The same paragraph mapped as "¶2: evidence for the traditional view" answers structure questions, tells you where to look for the sales figures, and takes fewer words.
>
> If your map contains a proper noun, you are probably recording content.

## Key takeaways

- Map function, not content: one phrase per paragraph plus whose view it carries.
- Mark pivots, attribution shifts, evaluative language, and lists. Nothing else.
- Recognise the passage shape early; it predicts where the author stands.
- Calibrate attitude by degree — most authors are qualified, and extreme choices are usually wrong.
- Predicting the question set from the map is worth about a minute per passage.
