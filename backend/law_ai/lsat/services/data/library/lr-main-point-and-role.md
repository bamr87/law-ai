## One skill, four stems

Structure questions do not ask whether the argument is any good. They ask what it *is*: which sentence is the conclusion, what job a particular sentence does, how the author proceeds, or where two speakers disagree. All four are descriptive, and all four are solved by the analysis in [[article:argument-anatomy]].

- **Main conclusion.** *The main point of the argument is that…* See [[concept:main-conclusion]].
- **Role of a claim.** *The statement that X plays which one of the following roles?* See [[concept:role-of-claim]].
- **Method of reasoning.** *The argument proceeds by…* See [[concept:method-of-reasoning]].
- **Point at issue.** *The dialogue most supports the claim that they disagree about whether…* See [[concept:point-at-issue]].

## Main conclusion

Run the why-test, then check for the subsidiary-conclusion trap: a claim that has support *and* gives support is not the main point. The main conclusion is the one nothing else in the stimulus is trying to establish.

Two structural tells worth memorising. When a stimulus opens with an attributed view — *"Some economists argue that…"* — the author's conclusion is almost always the rejection that follows. And when a pivot word appears, the conclusion is usually after it.

Credited answers on this subtype are paraphrases, not quotations. A choice that repeats the conclusion verbatim is not automatically right, and one that restates it in different words is not automatically wrong.

## Role of a claim

You are given one sentence and asked what it does. The workable method is to classify it *before* reading the choices, in three steps: is it the main conclusion, does it support something, or is it supported by something? Then check whether the author endorses it — a claim can be a premise for a view the author is about to reject.

> [!example] A claim the author does not accept
> *Critics of the transit levy say it will burden low-income households. This concern is not unreasonable: the levy is regressive on its face. But the levy funds free passes for exactly those households, so the net effect is progressive. The levy should pass.*
>
> Role of "the levy is regressive on its face": it supports the critics' concern, which the author acknowledges and then overrides. It is **a premise offered in support of a position the author ultimately rejects** — not the conclusion, not a premise for the author's own conclusion, and not an irrelevant aside.
>
> Three of the five choices will describe it as supporting the author's conclusion. It supports the opposing one.

## Method of reasoning

Describe the *technique*, not the content. Common patterns, with their exam phrasing:

- Counterexample — *"rejects a generalisation by citing a case that does not conform to it"*
- Analogy — *"argues that two situations are relevantly similar"*
- Ruling out alternatives — *"eliminates competing explanations to establish the remaining one"*
- Appeal to a principle — *"applies a general rule to a particular case"*
- Reductio — *"shows that a position leads to an untenable consequence"*
- Distinction — *"draws a distinction between two things that had been treated alike"*

The credited answer must match on *every* clause. A choice that says "cites statistical evidence to undermine a causal claim" fails if the argument cited an anecdote, even if the rest fits.

## Point at issue

Two speakers, and you must find what they actually disagree about. The test is two-sided and both halves are required:

**Each speaker must have a position on it, and those positions must conflict.**

Most wrong answers fail one half: something only one speaker addressed, or something both mentioned and agreed about. Work by elimination — for each choice, ask "did speaker one take a stance? did speaker two? are they opposed?" — rather than by intuition about the overall tone of the exchange.

> [!trap] Disagreement about emphasis
> Two speakers can sound like they are arguing while agreeing on every proposition, differing only in what they consider important. A choice describing that difference in emphasis is not a point at issue, because neither speaker has denied what the other asserted. Look for a proposition one affirms and the other rejects.

## Key takeaways

- All four subtypes are descriptive: what the argument is, not whether it works.
- Main conclusion: run the why-test and reject subsidiary conclusions.
- Role: classify the sentence yourself first, and check whether the author endorses it.
- Method: match every clause of the description, including what kind of evidence was used.
- Point at issue: both speakers must have taken a stance, and the stances must conflict.
