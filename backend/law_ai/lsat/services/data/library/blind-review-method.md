## The problem it solves

Ordinary review — check the key, read the explanation for the ones you missed — teaches very little. Reading an explanation produces recognition ("yes, that makes sense") which feels like learning and is not. You have not practised the retrieval; you have watched someone else do it.

Worse, ordinary review cannot distinguish the two reasons you missed a question, and they need completely different fixes:

- You **did not know how** to do it. A method problem.
- You **knew how** and did not have time, or rushed. A pacing problem.

Blind review separates them cleanly, which is why it is worth the time it costs.

## The procedure

1. **Take the section under time.** Mark every question you are not fully certain about — a dot in the margin is enough. Do not skip this; the marking is what makes the method work.
2. **Do not check the answers.**
3. **Re-solve every marked question with unlimited time**, no clock, no key. Take as long as you want. Write down, for each one, what you now think the answer is and *why*, and why each other choice fails.
4. **Now score twice.** Your timed score and your blind-review score.
5. **Only then read explanations**, and only for questions you got wrong in blind review.

## Reading the gap

The distance between the two scores is the diagnosis.

| Timed | Blind review | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| 17/25 | 24/25 | You have the method; the clock is the problem | Pacing and triage work — [[article:pacing-and-time-management]] |
| 17/25 | 18/25 | Method problem; more time does not help | Drill that family untimed until accuracy rises |
| 17/25 | 25/25 | Method is solid; you are rushing decisions | Slow the early questions, bank time deliberately |

A large gap is *good news* — it means the points are already within reach and the fix is procedural. A small gap means the reasoning is not there yet, and no amount of pacing work will produce it.

> [!example] Two students, same score, opposite prescriptions
> Both score 17 of 25 on the same section.
>
> **Student A** blind-reviews to 24. Given time, they can do nearly everything. Their loss is entirely clock-driven — they spent four minutes on two hard questions and rushed the last six. Prescription: triage practice and checkpoint discipline. Drilling more assumption questions would waste a month.
>
> **Student B** blind-reviews to 18. Given unlimited time they still cannot do these questions. Their loss is method. Prescription: untimed drilling by family, starting with whichever family the error log flags. Pacing practice would only produce faster wrong answers.
>
> Identical scores. Opposite study plans. Nothing except blind review distinguishes them.

## The "why each wrong answer is wrong" rule

During blind review, for every question, write a sentence on each of the four wrong choices. This is the part people skip and it is where most of the value sits.

The reasons repeat — extreme language, new term, reversed relationship, true but irrelevant, half-right — and articulating them fifty times is what turns [[article:answer-choice-elimination]] from something you have read into something you do automatically.

> [!tip] What to write for a question you got right
> If you were certain and correct, write nothing and move on.
>
> If you were *uncertain* and correct — a marked question — treat it exactly like a miss. A lucky right answer is a miss that has not happened yet, and the marking system exists precisely to catch these. Students who only review actual errors leave their most unstable questions untouched.

> [!trap] Reviewing by reading explanations
> Reading a good explanation is pleasant, feels productive, and teaches close to nothing, because the hard part — generating the reasoning yourself — has been done for you.
>
> The explanation is a *check* on your blind-review reasoning, not a substitute for it. Read it after you have committed to an answer and written down why. If you find yourself reading explanations first, you have reverted to ordinary review.

## Time cost

Blind review roughly doubles the time a section costs: 35 minutes to take it, 30 to 45 to review it. That ratio is correct. A study plan that is 90% new questions and 10% review buys repetition of existing errors; see [[article:building-a-study-plan]].

## Key takeaways

- Mark uncertainty during the timed section; the marking makes everything else work.
- Re-solve marked questions untimed before looking at any key or explanation.
- Score twice. The gap diagnoses method problems versus pacing problems.
- Write why each wrong choice fails — the reasons repeat, and articulating them builds the reflex.
- Review time should roughly equal drilling time. Explanations come last, as a check.
