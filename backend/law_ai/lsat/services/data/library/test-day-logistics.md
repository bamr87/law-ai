## Rehearse the conditions, not just the content

Whatever your administration format — remotely proctored at home or at a test centre — the single highest-return preparation is that exam day contains no novelty. Every practice exam in phase 4 of [[article:building-a-study-plan]] should match the real thing on the variables you control: same start time, same chair, same scratch paper, same break length, no pausing between sections.

The reason is narrow and physical. Attention across four 35-minute sections is a trained capacity, and people who only ever drill single sections discover in section three that they have never held focus that long. That discovery costs more points than any question type.

> [!example] The section-three collapse
> A test taker averages 21 of 25 on drilled Logical Reasoning sections. On their first full four-section simulation they score 21, 20, and then 14. Nothing about the third section was harder. They had never once sat three in a row, and their accuracy fell off a cliff at the ninety-minute mark.
>
> Four full simulations later the third-section number is 19. The fix was not method. It was endurance, and it is only trainable by simulating.

## The controllables

- **Environment.** A cleared surface, a closed door, a phone in another room, and — for remote administration — a room that satisfies the proctoring rules *before* the day, checked once in advance rather than discovered at check-in.
- **Materials.** Whatever your format permits, use exactly that in practice. If you get five sheets of scratch paper, practise with five, because running out mid-game is a scenario you want to have already met.
- **Body.** Eat something with slow carbohydrates ninety minutes before. Caffeine at your normal dose — exam day is the worst possible morning to change it. Hydrate to the point of comfort and no further; the break is short.
- **Arrival.** Be checked in early. Rushed check-in raises heart rate, and elevated arousal in section one is worth real points.

## The break

There is one scheduled break, and it is the only structural reset the exam gives you. Use it deliberately:

- Stand up and move. Circulation matters more than review.
- Eat something small if you brought it.
- Do **not** try to recall questions from the first half. It changes nothing and it primes rumination.
- Do not check the time obsessively; the proctor manages it.

> [!trap] The mid-exam autopsy
> The most expensive habit on test day is grading yourself while the exam is still running. "That section went badly" is a judgement you cannot make accurately — every experienced test taker has felt a section go badly and scored well on it — and it is a judgement that damages the sections still to come.
>
> The rule is: **the section is over when it is over.** No replaying, no estimating, no adjusting your plan for section four based on a feeling about section two. There is nothing to gain and a full section to lose.

## When something goes wrong

Occasionally something genuinely breaks: a proctor interruption, a technical fault, a fire alarm. Two things matter.

First, follow the reporting procedure immediately and in writing. Complaints raised on the day carry weight that complaints raised a week later do not.

Second, and harder: **do not abandon the exam in your head.** A three-minute interruption is a three-minute interruption. It is not a ruined score unless you spend the following section deciding it was one. The decision to keep working at full effort after something goes wrong is worth more points than any technique in this library.

## Key takeaways

- Rehearse the conditions: same start time, same materials, four sections with no pausing.
- Endurance across three-plus sections is a separate trainable skill from accuracy on one.
- Set up and verify the environment in advance; never discover a proctoring problem at check-in.
- Use the break to move and eat, never to review the first half.
- No mid-exam self-grading. Feelings about a section are uncorrelated with its score and they damage the next one.
