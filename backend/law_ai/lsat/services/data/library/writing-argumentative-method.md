## The structure

Five paragraphs, each with one job. This is not the only workable shape, but it is the one that reliably fits 35 minutes and that a reader can follow at speed.

1. **Thesis.** Name the position and the decision rule you are applying. Two to four sentences.
2. **Strongest reason.** One reason, developed — fact from the scenario, the warrant connecting it to your position, and the consequence.
3. **Second reason.** Same shape, different ground. If reason two is a restatement of reason one, cut it and develop reason one further.
4. **Counterargument and response.** The best argument against you, stated fairly, then answered.
5. **Close.** Not a summary. One or two sentences on why, weighing everything, the decision still goes your way.

## Fact, warrant, consequence

The commonest weakness in a writing sample is assertion without connection. Every reason paragraph should contain three parts:

```
Fact:        The scenario states the district exceeds the citywide
             average for green space per resident.
Warrant:     Public investment should go where the deficiency is
             greatest, not where the adequacy can be improved.
Consequence: So the marginal value of another park here is lower
             than the marginal value of housing.
```

The **warrant** is the part people skip, and it is the part that makes the paragraph an argument rather than an opinion. It is the same object as an assumption in [[article:lr-assumption-family]] — the unstated principle linking evidence to conclusion — except here you are expected to state it. See [[concept:warrant]].

## The concession that strengthens

A well-made concession makes your position stronger, because it shows you understood the cost and chose anyway. A badly-made one hands the essay away.

- **Strong:** concede the fact, deny its weight. *"Green space genuinely improves health outcomes, and the park would deliver that. But the scenario describes a housing shortage as acute and green space as adequate, and severity governs when the two compete."*
- **Weak:** concede the conclusion. *"The park might well be the better choice in some respects, and reasonable people could prefer it."* You have just written the other side's thesis into your own essay.

The rule: **concede facts, never the criterion.** Grant that the other side's evidence is real, then explain why it does not decide the question. See [[concept:concession-and-scope]].

> [!example] A counterargument paragraph that works
> *"The strongest case for the park is that green space is effectively irreversible once built over: a lot converted to housing will not become a park in this generation, while housing can be built on other sites. That asymmetry is real and it is the best argument against my position.*
>
> *It does not carry the day here for two reasons. First, the scenario identifies no comparable site — the alternative is not 'housing later elsewhere' but 'housing later, unspecified'. Second, the irreversibility argument proves too much: applied consistently it would block any development on any vacant land, which is not a decision rule the city could actually adopt."*
>
> Note what this does. It states the objection at full strength, in the opponent's best terms. Then it answers on the record — with a fact from the scenario and a reductio. A reader finishes the paragraph more convinced, not less. See [[concept:counterargument-response]].

## Self-scoring

You will not get a score, so build the feedback loop yourself. After drafting, read your essay once against these questions:

| Check | Failing looks like |
|---|---|
| Is my position in the first three sentences? | The reader learns it in paragraph three |
| Does each reason have a stated warrant? | Facts listed, connection implied |
| Did I state the objection at full strength? | A weak version I built to knock down |
| Did I concede a fact or the criterion? | "Reasonable people could prefer the park" |
| Does every paragraph do one job? | Two arguments tangled in one paragraph |
| Would a reader know my decision rule? | Reasons stacked with no weighing principle |

Doing this on three practice essays is worth more than writing ten unreviewed ones — the same logic as [[article:blind-review-method]].

> [!trap] Padding
> Length is not scored, and padding is visible. Repeating the thesis in different words, listing considerations you do not develop, and writing a summary conclusion that adds nothing all cost credibility with a reader who has seen thousands of these.
>
> A tight 450-word essay that argues one position well reads better than an 800-word essay that covers more ground shallowly.

## Key takeaways

- Five paragraphs: thesis with a decision rule, two developed reasons, counterargument and response, close.
- Every reason needs fact, warrant, and consequence — the warrant is the part people skip.
- Concede facts, never the criterion. State the objection at its strongest.
- Self-score against a fixed checklist; three reviewed essays beat ten unreviewed.
- Length earns nothing. Padding is visible and costs credibility.
