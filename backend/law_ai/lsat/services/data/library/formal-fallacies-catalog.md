## Why a catalog helps

Flaw questions, parallel-flaw questions, and a large share of weaken and assumption questions all reward the same skill: naming the defect in your own words before you read the choices. Naming is easier when you have a vocabulary, and the exam reuses a small set of defects far more often than a list of "logical fallacies" would suggest. See [[concept:flaw-in-reasoning]].

The credited answer will describe the defect in abstract terms — "takes for granted that", "confuses a necessary condition with a sufficient one", "draws a general conclusion from an unrepresentative sample". Matching your own plain-language name to that abstraction is the whole task.

## The conditional errors

- **Necessary–sufficient confusion.** Treats something required as something that guarantees, or vice versa. Answer phrasing: *"treats a condition that is necessary as if it were sufficient."* The single most common named flaw on the exam.
- **Affirming the consequent / denying the antecedent.** The two invalid conditional moves from [[article:conditional-logic-foundations]]. Answer phrasing: *"infers that because a condition is met, its cause must have occurred."*
- **Illicit reversal.** Takes `A → B` and uses `B → A`.

## The causal errors

- **Correlation to cause.** Answer phrasing: *"infers a causal relationship from a mere correlation."*
- **Ignoring an alternative explanation.** Answer phrasing: *"fails to consider that another factor may account for the observed effect."*
- **Reversing cause and effect.** Answer phrasing: *"mistakes an effect for its cause."*
- **Post hoc.** Treats sequence as causation because one thing followed another.

## The evidentiary errors

- **Unrepresentative sample.** Generalises from a group chosen in a way that does not represent the whole. See [[concept:sampling-flaw]].
- **Appeal to ignorance.** Treats the absence of evidence for a claim as evidence that it is false, or absence of disproof as proof. See [[concept:appeal-to-ignorance]].
- **Percentage and number confusion.** Slides between a rate and a count. See [[concept:percent-vs-number]].
- **Appeal to inappropriate authority or popularity.** Rests a factual claim on who said it or how many believe it.

## The language and structure errors

- **Equivocation.** One word carries two meanings across the argument, and the argument only works if you do not notice. Answer phrasing: *"relies on a term that is used in different senses."* See [[concept:equivocation]].
- **Circular reasoning.** The conclusion appears among the premises, restated. Answer phrasing: *"presupposes what it sets out to establish."* See [[concept:circular-reasoning]].
- **Straw man.** Attacks a position the opponent did not take.
- **Ad hominem.** Attacks the source rather than the claim.
- **Part–whole (composition and division).** Infers from properties of parts to the whole, or the reverse.
- **False dilemma.** Presents two options as exhaustive when others exist.
- **Conflating what is with what ought to be.** Moves from a description to a prescription without a bridging principle.

> [!example] Naming before reading
> *The commission's report cannot be trusted. Two of its seven authors previously consulted for the utility companies whose rates the report evaluates.*
>
> Name it first: the argument rejects a claim by pointing at who made it, without engaging the report's content — and it generalises from two authors to the whole document. That is an ad hominem with a part-to-whole move stacked on it.
>
> Now the credited answer is findable: *"rejects a position on the basis of the affiliations of some of those who advanced it, without addressing the reasoning offered in support of it."* Had you gone straight to the choices, three of them would have sounded reasonable.

> [!trap] Half-right flaw answers
> The most dangerous distractor on a flaw question describes something the argument genuinely does — but not its defect. *"Relies on the testimony of experts"* may be perfectly accurate and completely beside the point. The test is not "is this true of the argument" but "is this why the argument fails". Run every surviving choice against that question; [[article:lr-flaw-family]] has the full method.

## Key takeaways

- The exam reuses a small catalog of defects. Learn the names and the abstract phrasings.
- Necessary–sufficient confusion and correlation-to-cause are the two most frequent.
- Name the flaw in plain language before reading the choices; then match to the abstraction.
- Credited answers describe *why the argument fails*, not merely what it does.
- Half-right distractors are accurate descriptions of irrelevant features — the standard trap.
