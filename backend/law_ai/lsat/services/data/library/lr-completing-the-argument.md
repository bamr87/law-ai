## What the blank is for

The stimulus ends mid-sentence and the stem reads *which one of the following most logically completes the argument?* This looks like its own family but it is really a delivery mechanism: the blank can sit where a conclusion goes, where a premise goes, or where an explanation goes, and the sentence leading into it tells you which. See [[concept:completing-the-argument]].

Roughly three quarters of the time the blank takes a **conclusion**, which makes this family a close relative of the inference questions in [[article:lr-inference-family]] — with the important difference that the credited answer only has to be well supported, not airtight.

## Read the connector

The words immediately before the blank are the instruction. Read them first and read them carefully.

| Connector before the blank | What belongs there |
|---|---|
| *therefore*, *thus*, *so*, *hence*, *it follows that* | A conclusion drawn from what precedes |
| *because*, *since*, *for*, *given that* | A premise that supports what precedes |
| *unless*, *only if* | A condition, in the right logical direction |
| *however*, *but*, *yet* | Something that cuts against what precedes |
| *this is because* | An explanation of the preceding claim |

Getting this wrong is the family's characteristic error: supplying a perfectly good conclusion when the connector called for a premise produces a sentence that is true, on-topic, and grammatically impossible in the slot.

> [!example] The connector decides everything
> *Municipal libraries that extended their evening hours saw circulation rise by 15%, while those that kept standard hours saw no change. The increase cannot be attributed to a general rise in reading, because ______*
>
> The connector is *because*, so the blank is a **premise supporting** the claim that the rise is not a general trend.
>
> Correct shape: *"libraries that kept standard hours drew from the same neighbourhoods and saw no increase at all."* That is evidence for the claim.
>
> Wrong shape, and highly attractive: *"the extended hours caused the increase in circulation."* That is a fine conclusion and a terrible premise — it restates what the author is trying to establish rather than supporting it, which makes the argument circular. See [[concept:circular-reasoning]].

## Method

1. **Read the connector first**, then decide what kind of sentence the blank takes.
2. **Read the stimulus for the gap.** What is the argument missing to get where it is going?
3. **Pre-phrase the missing piece** in your own words.
4. **Eliminate on shape before content** — a choice of the wrong kind is wrong even if it is true.

Step 4 is where the time is saved. On a *because* blank, every choice that reads like a conclusion can go without evaluating its content at all.

> [!trap] The true-but-inert choice
> Because the stimulus is incomplete, several choices will be consistent with it and add nothing. They restate a premise, or they assert something plausible about the topic that the argument does not need.
>
> The check: read the completed argument aloud with the choice in the blank. If the argument does not get stronger or does not now reach its conclusion, the choice is inert — and inert is wrong, however true.

## When the blank takes a conclusion

Treat it as a most-strongly-supported question. The standard is not certainty; it is that the choice is better supported by the stimulus than the other four. Prefer modest wording, prefer the choice you can trace to specific text, and be suspicious of anything introducing a new term that appeared nowhere above the blank.

## Key takeaways

- The connector immediately before the blank tells you what kind of sentence belongs there.
- Most blanks take a conclusion; a substantial minority take a premise or an explanation.
- Eliminate on sentence type before evaluating content — it removes several choices instantly.
- A premise that restates the conclusion makes the argument circular; that is a trap, not an answer.
- On conclusion blanks, apply most-strongly-supported standards: modest, traceable, no new terms.
