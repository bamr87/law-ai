## The three parts

Almost every Logical Reasoning stimulus is built from three kinds of sentence, and telling them apart is the prerequisite for every other technique in this library.

- **Conclusion** — the claim the author wants you to accept. There is exactly one main conclusion.
- **Premise** — a claim offered as a reason to accept something else.
- **Background** — context that orients you but supports nothing. Frequently the first sentence.

A fourth appears in harder stimuli: the **subsidiary conclusion**, a claim that is supported by a premise *and* supports the main conclusion. It is the single most common wrong answer on main-conclusion questions, because it genuinely is a conclusion — just not the one being asked for. See [[concept:main-conclusion]].

## The "why" test

Indicator words help — *therefore*, *thus*, *so*, *hence*, *consequently* point forward to a conclusion; *because*, *since*, *for*, *given that*, *after all* point backward to a premise — but they are absent or misleading often enough that they cannot be your primary tool.

The reliable method is a question. Take the sentence you suspect is the conclusion and ask: **why does the author think this?** If the rest of the stimulus answers, you have the conclusion. Then reverse it: take a candidate premise and ask **what does this get me?** If the answer is another sentence in the stimulus, you are holding a premise.

> [!example] Running the test
> *Municipal composting programmes reduce landfill volume. The city's new programme diverted 4,000 tonnes last year. Critics note the programme costs more per tonne than landfilling. But cost per tonne is the wrong measure, because landfill capacity is finite and replacing it is far more expensive than any composting subsidy. The programme should be expanded.*
>
> Test "The programme should be expanded." Why does the author think so? Because cost per tonne is the wrong measure, because capacity is finite. The rest of the stimulus answers — so it is the conclusion.
>
> Test "Cost per tonne is the wrong measure." Why? Because capacity is finite. That also has support — so it is a *subsidiary* conclusion, supported by one thing and supporting another. On a main-conclusion question this is the trap answer, and it will look extremely attractive.

## Structural signals worth knowing

- **The pivot.** *But*, *however*, *yet*, *nevertheless* very often mark the sentence *after* which the author's own view begins. Everything before is frequently someone else's position being set up for rejection.
- **Attributed views.** *Some argue*, *critics contend*, *it is widely believed* introduce a position the author is usually about to attack. Mistaking an attributed view for the author's conclusion is a top-three beginner error.
- **The imperative or evaluative sentence.** *Should*, *ought*, *must*, *is mistaken*, *is unjustified* — recommendations and judgements are conclusions far more often than they are premises.

> [!trap] The last-sentence assumption
> Beginners reach for the final sentence. On easy items that works often enough to feel like a rule, which is precisely what makes it dangerous: the exam writes hard items where the conclusion sits first or in the middle, and the last sentence is a restated premise or an implication the author is *not* claiming. Run the why-test even when the last sentence looks obvious.

## Why this is the foundation

Once you can locate the conclusion, the rest of Logical Reasoning becomes tractable:

- An **assumption** is an unstated claim the argument needs to get from premises to *that* conclusion — see [[article:lr-assumption-family]].
- To **weaken** is to attack the support for *that* conclusion, not to contradict a premise — see [[article:lr-strengthen-and-weaken]].
- A **flaw** is a defect in the move from premises to *that* conclusion — see [[article:lr-flaw-family]].
- A **role** question asks what one sentence does relative to *that* conclusion — see [[article:lr-main-point-and-role]].

Every one of those definitions has "that conclusion" in it. Get the conclusion wrong and the correct answer becomes unfindable no matter how good your technique is.

## Key takeaways

- Three sentence roles: conclusion, premise, background — plus subsidiary conclusions in harder items.
- Indicator words help but are not reliable; the why-test is.
- Attributed views and the sentence before a pivot are usually not the author's conclusion.
- Subsidiary conclusions are the classic main-point trap: supported *and* supporting.
- Every other LR technique is defined relative to the conclusion, so this step is not optional.
