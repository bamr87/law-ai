## Paradox: both facts are true

*Which one of the following, if true, most helps to resolve the apparent discrepancy?* The stimulus presents two facts that sit uncomfortably together. Your job is to add a third fact that makes both comfortable — **not** to deny either one. See [[concept:resolve-paradox]].

The single most useful habit: **state the tension in one sentence before reading the choices.** "Sales rose while the number of customers fell" or "the drug worked in the lab and failed in the field". Once the tension is named, the credited answer is usually the one obvious thing that would explain it.

## The standard resolutions

The exam reuses a small set of explanations:

- **A hidden third group.** The two facts are about different populations that were treated as one.
- **A change in composition.** The average moved because the mix changed, not because any individual did.
- **Rate versus count.** A percentage and an absolute number moved in opposite directions because the base changed. See [[article:lr-numbers-and-percentages]].
- **A different mechanism.** Both facts are true because something else intervened between them.
- **A timing difference.** The two measurements cover different periods, or one lags the other.

> [!example] Naming the tension first
> *Since the hospital introduced its rapid-triage protocol, the average time from arrival to treatment has increased by eleven minutes. Yet patient outcomes have improved substantially, and the protocol is widely credited for the improvement.*
>
> Tension in one sentence: *waiting longer, but doing better.*
>
> Now generate: perhaps the protocol identifies low-urgency patients and deliberately delays them, so the average rose while the urgent cases are seen faster. That is a composition explanation, and a choice reading *"the protocol routes non-urgent patients to a separate queue, reducing the wait for critical cases"* resolves it exactly.
>
> Note what does **not** resolve it: a choice arguing outcomes did not really improve. That denies a premise, which is never the job here.

> [!trap] Deepening instead of resolving
> Half the wrong answers in this family make the discrepancy *worse* — they explain why one of the two facts is even more surprising than it looked. Under time pressure these read as "relevant" and get picked. After choosing, reread the two facts with your answer inserted and ask whether they now sit comfortably. If you still feel the tension, you picked a deepener.

## Evaluate: what would settle it?

*Which one of the following would be most useful to know in evaluating the argument?* You are not strengthening or weakening. You are naming the **question whose answer decides the case**. See [[concept:evaluate-the-argument]].

The mechanical tool is the **variance test**. For each choice, imagine the two extreme answers:

- If the answer is *yes* (or *high*), does the argument get notably stronger?
- If the answer is *no* (or *low*), does it get notably weaker?

A choice passes only if both directions move the argument, and in opposite directions. A choice whose answer leaves the argument unchanged either way is the standard distractor, no matter how on-topic it sounds.

```
Argument: the new fertiliser caused the yield increase.
Choice:   "Whether neighbouring farms that did not use the fertiliser
           saw similar yield increases."
  Yes → neighbours also gained → alternative cause → argument weakens
  No  → only treated farms gained            → argument strengthens
  Both directions move it. Passes.
```

Because evaluate questions are usually built on causal stimuli, the variance test almost always lands on one of the four attacks in [[article:causal-reasoning-foundations]] — most often ruling an alternative cause in or out.

## Key takeaways

- Paradox: both stated facts are true. Add a third that reconciles them; never deny one.
- Name the tension in one sentence before reading the choices.
- Standard resolutions: hidden group, composition change, rate versus count, different mechanism, timing.
- Evaluate: find the question whose answer decides the argument, using the variance test.
- A choice that leaves the argument unchanged either way fails, however relevant it sounds.
